About past papers

FIFA does not publish past papers or mock exams, and its Exam Rules ban candidates from copying or sharing questions. Each candidate gets a unique set drawn from a question database. Sites that advertise "real past papers" are selling their own questions.

So the practice bank here is written for this course, in the exam's format, with each answer tied to a regulation. The Sources tab links free third-party mocks for more volume.

The licensing cycle

FIFA had not announced 2027 dates when this was built. The dates below are the 2026 cycle, from FIFA Circular 1956 and FIFA's licensing page. Expect a similar calendar and check agents.fifa.com from January.

  1. Now
    Study the regulations

    Work through the exam course. Download the newest Study Materials as soon as FIFA posts them, because the revised RSTP takes effect on 1 January 2027.

  2. 20 Jan – 6 Mar
    Apply on the FIFA Agent Platform

    A 45-day window. Choose your member association, upload ID, declare eligibility, pay the exam fee shown on the platform. Miss it and you wait a year.

    Results

    Released within 30 days of the last session, with the correct answers. A failed attempt can be reviewed for three days.

  6. +90 days
    Pay the licence fee

    Pay within 90 days of passing or the application lapses. Then keep up the annual fee and CPD.

Lesson 1 · Start here

How the exam works

Most people who fail know the rules roughly. The format punishes "roughly", so learn the format first.

The format, from FIFA's own documents

  • 20 multiple-choice questions in 60 minutes. Each is worth 5%. The pass mark is 75%, which is 15 questions.
  • One or more answers may be correct. Only a fully correct answer scores. Select one of two correct options and you get zero.
  • All questions appear on a single page. Answers submit automatically when the time runs out.
  • Languages: English, French, German or Spanish. Every candidate gets a different set from the same database.
  • Some questions are short case studies that need a calculation. A calculator is built into the platform.

It is open book, with one book

You may consult the Study Materials in digital form inside the exam environment. Nothing else: no printed notes, no other websites, no second screen. You may have a blank sheet of paper and a pen, which the invigilator checks and has you destroy afterwards.

Three minutes a question is not enough to look everything up. The skill to build is knowing which document and article holds the answer, then confirming it quickly.

What the Study Materials contain

  • FIFA Football Agent Regulations FFAR
  • Regulations on the Status and Transfer of Players RSTP
  • FIFA Clearing House Regulations
  • FIFA Statutes, Code of Ethics, Disciplinary Code
  • Other material such as case studies, the FIFA Guardians Child Safeguarding Toolkit and the Ethical Recruitment Guide
Trap

FIFA can add material and updates the pack. Always study from the newest edition on the Agent Platform. For the next exam that matters more than usual, because a revised RSTP enters into force on 1 January 2027.

On the day

  • Two devices: a laptop or desktop with camera and microphone for the exam, and a smartphone as a second camera for invigilation.
  • A private, quiet room with nobody else in it. No VPN. Extra monitors switched off and turned away.
  • The same ID document you used to apply.
  • No breaks, including toilet breaks. Leaving counts as withdrawing.
  • If your connection or device fails, the attempt is over and the application is rejected. Use a wired or very reliable connection and keep the charger plugged in.
Read these two yourself

The Football Agent Exam Rules and "Information on the Licensing Process and the FIFA Football Agent Exam" are short and answer nearly every practical question. Both are linked in Sources.

How hard is it?

FIFA's Circular 1956 says 7,745 candidates sat the 2025 edition. Exam-prep sites report a pass rate of about 18% for that sitting and 52% for the first exam in 2023. Those pass rates are third-party figures. Either way, plan for it as a serious exam.

Lesson 2 · Foundations

FIFA's structure and Statutes

A handful of questions come from the Statutes. They are easy marks if you know who does what.

The pyramid

  • FIFA is an association under Swiss law, based in Zurich.
  • 211 member associations, one per country as a rule. Six confederations: AFC, CAF, Concacaf, CONMEBOL, OFC, UEFA.
  • Clubs, leagues and players are bound through their member association, which must make them comply with FIFA's statutes, regulations and decisions.

The bodies

BodyRole
CongressSupreme and legislative body. One vote per member association. Adopts and amends the Statutes.
CouncilStrategic and oversight body. Approves regulations such as the FFAR and RSTP.
General secretariatExecutive, operational and administrative body, led by the Secretary General.
Judicial bodiesDisciplinary Committee, Ethics Committee, Appeal Committee.
Football TribunalDecides football disputes: Dispute Resolution Chamber, Players' Status Chamber, Agents Chamber.

Disputes

  • The Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) in Lausanne hears appeals against final FIFA decisions. The deadline is 21 days from receipt of the decision.
  • Recourse to ordinary courts is prohibited unless FIFA regulations specifically provide for it.
  • The Laws of the Game belong to IFAB, not FIFA.
Trap

CAS and the Football Tribunal are not "judicial bodies" in the Statutes' sense. A select-all question on judicial bodies has exactly three right answers.

Find it fast

Statutes: chapter on organisation for the bodies, the articles near the end for CAS and the ban on ordinary courts. Skim the definitions page too; "official", "player", "league" and "association" are defined there.

Lesson 3 · FFAR

Getting and keeping the licence

The FFAR is the heart of the exam. Expect more questions from it than from any other single document.

Scope and vocabulary

  • The FFAR governs representation agreements with an international dimension: linked to an international transfer, or covering several transactions of which one is international. Purely domestic work falls under national regulations, which associations must align with the FFAR. FFAR Art. 2–3
  • Client: a player, coach, club, member association or single-entity league. Individual: a player or coach. Engaging Entity: the club or association signing the Individual. Releasing Entity: the club they leave.
  • Football Agent Services: negotiation and related activity aimed at concluding a Transaction. Only a licensed agent may perform them.
  • Approach: any contact with a potential client about services, whether in person, electronic or through someone else.

The path to a licence FFAR Art. 4–8

  1. Apply through the FIFA Agent Platform during the application window.
  2. Meet the eligibility requirements at application and at all times afterwards.
  3. Pass the exam.
  4. Pay the annual fee within 90 days of passing.

The licence is issued for an indefinite period, is strictly personal and non-transferable, and lets the agent work worldwide.

Eligibility FFAR Art. 5

Learn this list well. It is a favourite for select-all questions. The applicant must:

  • make no false or misleading statements in the application;
  • never have been convicted of, or settled, a charge involving organised crime, drug trafficking, corruption, bribery, money laundering, tax evasion, fraud, match manipulation, misappropriation, breach of fiduciary duty, forgery, legal malpractice, sexual abuse, violent crime, harassment, exploitation, or trafficking of children or vulnerable young adults;
  • never have been suspended for two years or more, disqualified or struck off by a regulator or sports body for ethics or conduct breaches;
  • not be an official or employee of FIFA, a confederation, a member association, a league, a club, or a body representing clubs or leagues;
  • hold no interest in a club, academy or league;
  • not have performed agent services without a licence in the 24 months before applying;
  • not have been declared bankrupt, or been majority shareholder of a business that went bankrupt or into liquidation, in the five years before applying;
  • not have held an interest in a sports betting business in the 12 months before applying.

There is no education requirement. Lawyers, accountants and former players get no exemption. The only route around the exam is a national-law licensing system that FIFA has approved. FFAR Art. 23

Staying licensed

  • Pay the annual fee, complete CPD every year, keep meeting Art. 5. FFAR Art. 7, 9
  • Falling short triggers an automatic provisional suspension, and withdrawal if it is not fixed in time. FFAR Art. 17–18
  • Agents licensed under FIFA's older regulations (1991, 1994, 2001, 2008) had a one-off route back in without the exam. FFAR Art. 22
Trap

Note the different look-back periods: 24 months for unlicensed activity, five years for bankruptcy, 12 months for betting interests. Questions swap them around.

Lesson 4 · FFAR

Representation agreements and minors

This is your working contract as an agent. The exam tests it, and you will draft one within weeks of being licensed.

The agreement FFAR Art. 12

  • Written, and signed before any services are performed.
  • Minimum content: names of the parties, duration, the service fee, the nature of the services, signatures.
  • With a player or coach: maximum two years. Extension only by a new agreement. An automatic renewal clause is null and void.
  • With a club or association: no maximum term.
  • Before signing with an Individual, tell them in writing to consider independent legal advice, and get written confirmation that they took it or declined.
  • Exclusivity is allowed. A clause that stops the Individual negotiating their own employment contract without you, or penalises them for it, is null and void.
  • Either side may terminate at any time for just cause.

One side per deal, with one exception

As a rule an agent acts for one party in a transaction. The single permitted dual representation is the Individual and the Engaging Entity together, with the prior explicit written consent of both. Acting for the Releasing Entity alongside anyone else in the same deal is prohibited, and "connected" agents cannot be used to get around it. FFAR Art. 12(8)–(10)

Running an agency FFAR Art. 11

You may trade through a company. You remain personally responsible for what its staff do, and staff without a licence may not approach clients or perform agent services.

Minors FFAR Art. 13

  • No approach to a minor or their guardian until the six months before the age at which the player can sign a first professional contract in the country of employment.
  • The guardian's prior written consent is required for the approach.
  • You must first complete the CPD course on minors.
  • The guardian signs the representation agreement with the minor.
  • A fee is only due when the player signs a first or later professional contract.
Suspended, still in the text

The dual representation paragraphs are among the provisions FIFA suspended worldwide by Circular 1873 on 30 December 2023. They remain printed in the FFAR, so learn them as written and know they are suspended.

Trap

"Two years" is for Individuals only. A question about an agreement with a club that lasts four years is testing whether you over-apply the limit.

Lesson 5 · FFAR

Service fees and the cap

The calculation questions live here. They are quick marks once you have done ten by hand.

The cap as written FFAR Art. 15

ClientRemuneration up to USD 200,000 a yearPortion above USD 200,000
Individual5%3%
Engaging Entity5%3%
Individual + Engaging Entity (permitted dual representation)10%6%
Releasing Entity10% of the transfer compensation

Worked example

You act for the player only. Annual remuneration is USD 500,000.

  • First USD 200,000 at 5% = USD 10,000
  • Remaining USD 300,000 at 3% = USD 9,000
  • Maximum fee = USD 19,000 a year

The higher band applies to the excess only. Applying 3% to the whole salary is the usual wrong answer.

How fees are paid, as written FFAR Art. 14

  • The fee is based on the Individual's remuneration for an Individual or Engaging Entity, and on the transfer compensation for a Releasing Entity. Check the definition of Remuneration for what counts.
  • Client pays: each client pays their own agent. Exception: the Engaging Entity may pay on the Individual's behalf where annual remuneration is under USD 200,000.
  • Payment comes after the registration period closes, in instalments across the life of the employment contract, pro rata to what the player actually receives.
  • Payments were designed to run through the FIFA Clearing House.
Where the law stands

A German court injunction in May 2023 led FIFA to suspend the fee cap and related rules worldwide through Circular 1873 on 30 December 2023. Commentators list the suspended rules as: the cap, the payment and timing rules, client pays, payment through the Clearing House, the ban on double representation, some reporting duties, disclosure and publication, and the rule submitting agents to FIFA jurisdiction.

On 16 July 2026 the EU Court of Justice ruled in RRC Sports (C-209/23). It did not strike the cap down and did not approve it; it left justification to the national court. It did signal that the rule on approaching clients under exclusive agreements, and blanket publication of sanctions and deals, do not fit EU law. FIFA said it would meet agent bodies. No reinstatement had been reported by late 2026.

For the exam: learn the rules as printed, know what is suspended, and read any circular included in your Study Materials.

Lesson 6 · FFAR

Duties, disputes and sanctions

What you may and may not do once licensed, and who decides when it goes wrong.

Approaching other agents' clients FFAR Art. 16(1)

As written, you may sign a client who has no exclusive agreement with another agent. A client under an exclusive agreement may only be approached, or signed, during the final two months of that agreement. This is the rule the CJEU criticised in July 2026, so watch for an amendment.

Things an agent must do FFAR Art. 16(2)

  • Keep meeting eligibility and comply with FIFA's statutes, regulations and decisions.
  • Act in the client's best interests, avoid conflicts of interest, and be honest with them.
  • Name yourself and your client in the transaction documents.
  • Upload representation agreements and report required information on the platform, within the set deadlines (partly suspended).
  • Complete CPD each year.

Things an agent must not do FFAR Art. 16(3)

  • Offer money, gifts or other benefits to a client, their family or friends to win a representation agreement.
  • Hold an interest in a player's transfer compensation or future transfer value.
  • Hold an interest in a club, academy or league.
  • Use unlicensed people or structures to get around the rules.

Disputes and discipline

  • Disputes from an international-dimension agreement: Agents Chamber of the Football Tribunal. Claims must be brought within two years. FFAR Art. 20
  • Breaches: FIFA Disciplinary Committee and, where relevant, the Ethics Committee. National matters go to the member association. FFAR Art. 21
  • Clients are bound too. A club or player who uses an unlicensed person can be sanctioned.
Find it fast

FFAR layout: Art. 1–3 scope, 4–10 licensing, 11–13 acting as an agent, 14–15 fees, 16 rights and obligations, 17–18 suspension and withdrawal, 19 disclosure, 20–21 disputes and discipline, 22 onwards transitional and national rules. Write this map on your blank sheet in the first minute of the exam.

Lesson 7 · RSTP

Status, registration, windows and loans

The RSTP is the rulebook for every transfer you will ever work on, and the second big source of questions.

New edition from 1 January 2027

FIFA approved a revised RSTP in June 2026 after talks with player, club and league bodies. Its main changes are in contract stability, covered in the next lesson. Article numbers here follow the edition in force through 2026. Check each one against the Study Materials for your exam.

Status RSTP Art. 2–4

  • A professional has a written contract and is paid more than their actual expenses. Everyone else is an amateur.
  • A professional can re-register as an amateur no sooner than 30 days after their last match as a professional.
  • A player who stops playing stays registered with their last club's association for 30 months.

Registration RSTP Art. 5–9

  • A player must be registered with an association to play for a club, and with one club at a time.
  • In a season: registered with at most three clubs, official matches for at most two. There is an exception for associations with overlapping seasons.
  • Two registration periods a year, fixed by each association. A professional whose contract ended before a window closed can be registered outside it.
  • The player passport records every club since the season of the 12th birthday. It drives training compensation and solidarity.
  • An international move needs an International Transfer Certificate (ITC), requested through the transfer matching system (TMS). No ITC is needed under age 10.

Loans RSTP Art. 10

  • A written agreement. Minimum length: the interval between two registration periods. Maximum: one year.
  • No sub-loans.
  • Since 1 July 2024: at most six professionals loaned out and six loaned in at any time in a season, and at most three each way with the same club.
  • Players aged 21 and under who are club-trained do not count towards the limits.

Overdue payables RSTP Art. 12bis

A club that is more than 30 days late on a contractual payment can be sanctioned, once the creditor has put it in default in writing with at least 10 days to pay. Sanctions run from a warning to a registration ban.

Trap

Three numbers that get mixed up: 30 days (amateur re-registration, overdue payables), 30 months (registration after retirement), 10 days (default notice for overdue payables) against 15 days (default notice for outstanding salaries, next lesson).

Lesson 8 · RSTP

Contract stability

Articles 13 to 18 decide when a contract can end and what it costs. They are the most tested part of the RSTP and the part that changes most in 2027.

The core rules RSTP Art. 13–16

  • A contract ends on expiry or by mutual agreement. Art. 13
  • Either party may terminate for just cause without consequences. Abusive conduct designed to force the other side out gives that side just cause. Art. 14
  • Outstanding salaries: two monthly salaries unpaid, plus a written default notice giving at least 15 days. Art. 14bis
  • Sporting just cause: an established professional who played in fewer than 10% of official matches may terminate within 15 days of the season's last official match. Art. 15
  • No unilateral termination during the season. Art. 16

Breach without just cause RSTP Art. 17

  • The party in breach pays compensation.
  • Player sanction in the protected period: four months out of official matches, six in aggravating circumstances.
  • Protected period, 2026 edition: three seasons or years for a contract signed before the 28th birthday, two if signed after.

Contract rules every agent uses RSTP Art. 18

  • If an agent is involved in negotiating a contract, they are named in it.
  • Minimum term: to the end of the season. Maximum: five years. Under-18s: three years.
  • A club must tell the player's current club in writing before negotiating. The player is free to sign elsewhere only if the contract has expired or expires within six months.
  • A contract cannot be conditional on a medical or a work permit.
What the January 2027 edition changes

These points come from law-firm commentary (Kaufman & Canoles, Withers) on the text FIFA approved in June 2026, following the EU court's Diarra judgment of 4 October 2024. Confirm each in the official text.

  • Art. 17 is retitled "Consequences of breach of contract". Parties may agree compensation in advance; the Football Tribunal can reduce an excessive amount and disregard a manifestly unfair one.
  • Without an agreed amount, compensation is the actual damage. As a principle each side gets at least the residual value of the contract. There is an express duty to mitigate.
  • A new club is jointly liable only if it induced the breach. Inducement is presumed, rebuttably, if the player signs within 45 days.
  • Club sanctions become an escalating ladder for repeat breaches rather than an automatic two-window ban. The player sanction of four to six months stays.
  • A penalty of up to six monthly salaries for abusive conduct, such as freezing a player out of training.
  • Protected-period lengths are reported to vary by age at signing, from four seasons under 23 down to one over 32.
  • Under-18s already registered with the club for a set time may sign for up to five years, with safeguards.
  • New Art. 21bis: on a permanent international transfer, a player on fixed pay under EUR 150,000 receives 5% of the fixed fee from the releasing club.
  • A former association can no longer block an ITC over a contract dispute.
Trap

The exam is set from one edition. Once the 2027 Study Materials are out, redo this lesson against that text and trust it over anything here.

Lesson 9 · RSTP

Protection of minors

Young players are where new agents usually start, and where the rules are strictest.

The rule RSTP Art. 19

International transfers are only allowed for players over 18. The same applies to the first registration of a foreign player who has not lived in the country for five continuous years.

The exceptions

  • Parents move to the new country for reasons not linked to football.
  • Aged 16 to 18 within the EU/EEA, or between two associations in the same country. The new club must provide football education, academic or vocational education, proper living arrangements and proof to its association.
  • Border rule: the player lives within 50 km of the border and the club is within 50 km of it on the other side. Maximum 100 km apart. The player keeps living at home.
  • Humanitarian: the player fled their country without parents and may stay in the new one, under strict conditions.
  • Exchange student on an academic programme, for up to one year, with an amateur club.

Process

  • Every international transfer or first registration of a minor aged 10 or over under an exception needs approval, applied for through TMS, before the ITC request. RSTP Art. 19(4)
  • Clubs must report minors attending their academies. RSTP Art. 19bis

How this meets the FFAR

RSTP Art. 19 controls whether the minor can move. FFAR Art. 13 controls whether you can approach and represent them. A case-study question may need both.

Trap

A club offering the parents jobs so the family can relocate is a move linked to football. The parents exception does not apply.

Lesson 10 · RSTP

Training compensation, solidarity and the Clearing House

Two different systems that reward training clubs. Questions test whether you can tell them apart and do the sums.

WhereArt. 20, Annexe 4Art. 21, Annexe 5
TriggerFirst registration as a professional, and each international transfer of a professional up to the end of the calendar year of the 23rd birthdayA professional transfers before their contract expires and a fee is paid, at any age

Training compensation details Annexe 4

  • Clubs are placed in categories 1 to 4 by their association. Each category has a yearly training cost per confederation.
  • The seasons of the 12th to 15th birthdays are charged at category 4 cost.
  • Not due if the former club terminated without just cause, if the player moves to a category 4 club, or if a professional becomes an amateur again on transfer.
  • Within the EU/EEA there are special rules, including that the former club must have offered a contract to keep its entitlement.

Solidarity split Annexe 5

  • Seasons of the 12th to 15th birthdays: 5% of the 5% each, which is 0.25% of the fee per season.
  • Seasons of the 16th to 23rd birthdays: 10% of the 5% each, which is 0.5% of the fee per season.
  • Example: fee EUR 10m, a club trained the player for the seasons of his 16th to 19th birthdays. 4 × 0.5% = 2% = EUR 200,000.

The FIFA Clearing House

  • A separate entity based in Paris that has processed training rewards since November 2022.
  • A trigger in TMS produces an Electronic Player Passport (EPP). Clubs and associations review it. FIFA then issues an Allocation Statement saying who is owed what.
  • The new club pays the Clearing House, which pays the training clubs after compliance checks.
Trap

Training compensation stops at 23. Solidarity has no age limit on the transfer itself; only the training years counted stop at 23. A 29-year-old's transfer still generates solidarity.

Lesson 11 · RSTP

National teams, coaches, women's football, third parties

Shorter topics that each tend to produce one question.

Release to national teams RSTP Annexe 1

  • Clubs must release players for the windows in the international match calendar and for final tournaments.
  • No financial compensation for the club. The association pays travel. The club insures the player for the release period.
  • A player may not refuse a call-up, and may not play for the club during the period they should have been released.

Coaches RSTP Annexe 2

Coaches have their own employment rules: written contracts, just cause, outstanding salaries of two months with a 15-day notice, and compensation for breach. Their disputes go to the Players' Status Chamber.

Female players RSTP Art. 18, 18quater

  • Maternity leave: at least 14 weeks, 8 of them after the birth, at two-thirds of salary.
  • A contract cannot depend on a player being or becoming pregnant. Dismissal for pregnancy is presumed to be without just cause, with extra compensation and sporting sanctions.
  • A player may be registered outside a window on returning from maternity leave, and a club may register temporary cover.
  • 2024 amendments added adoption and family leave and protections around menstrual health.

Third parties RSTP Art. 18bis, 18ter

  • No club may let anyone else influence its employment and transfer decisions.
  • No club or player may give a third party a share of a future transfer fee. Sell-on clauses in favour of a former club are fine, because a former club is not a third party.
  • The FFAR adds the same idea for agents: no interest in a client's transfer value.
Find it fast

Annexe 1 national teams, 2 coaches, 3 TMS and ITC procedure, 4 training compensation, 5 solidarity, 6 futsal. Confirm the numbering in the 2027 edition.

Lesson 12 · Enforcement

Football Tribunal, discipline and ethics

Know where each kind of dispute goes and what happens to people who ignore a decision.

Who hears what RSTP Art. 22–24; Procedural Rules

ChamberTypical cases
Dispute Resolution ChamberPlayer against club employment disputes with an international dimension, contract stability linked to an ITC, training compensation and solidarity
Players' Status ChamberClub against club, coach employment disputes, regulatory applications such as minors and eligibility to play for national teams
Agents ChamberDisputes under representation agreements with an international dimension
  • Time limit: two years from the event giving rise to the dispute.
  • Free of charge where a party is a player, coach, football agent or match agent. Clubs in dispute with each other pay costs and an advance.
  • Decisions can be issued without grounds; a party asks for the grounds within the set deadline, then has 21 days to appeal to CAS.
  • Players and clubs may instead use an independent national tribunal that meets FIFA's standards, if their contract says so.
  • Mediation is available and free.

Disciplinary Code

  • Applies to associations, clubs, officials, players, agents and match agents.
  • Sanctions range from warnings and fines to match suspensions, transfer bans, points deductions and bans from football activity.
  • Ignoring a money decision of FIFA or CAS brings a final deadline, then for clubs a registration ban until payment, with points deductions possible.
  • Also covers discrimination, match manipulation, forgery and doping.

Code of Ethics

  • Binds officials, players, agents and match agents.
  • Duties of neutrality, loyalty, confidentiality, reporting and cooperation. Rules on conflicts of interest, gifts, bribery, betting and protection of physical and mental integrity.
  • The Ethics Committee has an investigatory chamber and an adjudicatory chamber.
Trap

Limitation periods and minimum sanctions in these two codes are number-heavy and were amended recently. Look them up in the exam; do not answer them from memory.

Lesson 13 · Enforcement

Safeguarding and ethical recruitment

FIFA lists the Guardians toolkit and the Ethical Recruitment Guide as exam material. Few candidates read them.

FIFA Guardians toolkit

  • Written for member associations, to prevent harm to children in football and respond properly to concerns.
  • Built around five principles and five steps. Read them in the toolkit and learn their order.
  • Core ideas: act in the best interests of the child; children's rights apply to every child without discrimination; safeguarding is everyone's responsibility; roles must be defined; concerns must be reported and passed to the authorities where a crime may be involved.
  • Know the vocabulary: safeguarding against child protection, the types of abuse, the role of a safeguarding officer, safer recruitment of staff.

Ethical recruitment

  • No promises of trials or contracts in return for money from players or families.
  • Honest information about a club, country, salary and living conditions before anyone travels.
  • Proper visas, written contracts in a language the player understands, and a way home if it falls through.
  • Extra care for minors and players moving from poorer football economies.
How to study these

Read each once with the exam in mind, noting lists and definitions. In the exam, go straight to the document's contents page rather than trusting memory.

Lesson 14 · Finish

Exam technique and the final week

A routine for the 60 minutes, so nothing on the day is a decision.

The 60 minutes

  1. Minutes 0–2. On your blank sheet, write the document map: FFAR article ranges, RSTP article ranges and annexes.
  2. Minutes 2–25. One pass through all 20. Answer what you know. Mark anything that needs a lookup or a calculation.
  3. Minutes 25–50. Lookups, hardest last. Cap each at three minutes.
  4. Minutes 50–58. Re-read every question stem for "not", "except", "all", "minimum", "maximum".
  5. Minute 58. Submit. Do not let the clock do it for you mid-edit.

Select-all questions

  • Treat every option as its own true/false question.
  • One wrong tick costs the whole mark, so an option you cannot verify is worth a lookup.
  • Blank answers score the same as wrong ones. Never leave one empty.

Searching the Study Materials

  • Search for the rare word in the question, such as "bankrupt", "guardian", "sub-loan", "50 km".
  • Search numbers both ways: "two" and "2", "fifteen" and "15".

Before you start

What the job really is

The licence lets you work. It does not bring you a single client.

This track borrows its shape from how the John Viola Academy describes its own pathway: learn the fundamentals, get licensed, then build through mentoring and a network. Viola, one of the UK's early FIFA-licensed agents, is blunt about the order of importance.

"The practicalities part is much, much bigger than the theory."John Viola, interviewed by Flashscore
"It's not a get-rich-quick scheme that we're involved in. This is a serious business."John Viola, interviewed by Flashscore
"The player always comes first."John Viola, interviewed by FootballCritic, 2020

What agents spend their time on

  • Recruiting players. Watching football, building trust with players and families. Most of the early years.
  • Knowing clubs. Who decides, what they need, what they pay.
  • Doing deals. A few intense weeks a year around the windows.
  • Looking after clients. Viola's line is that the big deal should be a small part of the service. Contracts, relocation, form, injuries, life after football.

The economics, plainly

  • Fees are a percentage of salary, usually paid in instalments over the contract. A first deal may pay you months later.
  • A player on a modest salary generates a modest fee. Early income is thin and irregular.
  • Plan to fund yourself for 18 to 24 months, or keep other income. Check that any job you keep does not breach FFAR eligibility, such as employment by a club.

Before you start

Pick your lane

A new agent cannot compete everywhere. Choose a market small enough to know better than anyone.

Choose a niche on three axes

  • Geography. A league or region you can watch live every week. Local knowledge is the one edge big agencies lack.
  • Level. Second and third tiers, under-21 and B-team football, the women's game, futsal. Top-flight players already have agents.
  • Corridor. A route between two markets you understand, such as a country that produces players and a league that buys them. Language and culture matter here.

Learn your market's rules

  • Your national association's agent regulations and registration steps. Domestic deals follow these, not the FFAR.
  • Registration windows, squad size limits, homegrown and foreign-player quotas.
  • Work-permit rules for foreign players, which decide who a club can actually sign.
  • Typical salaries by division. Ask players, club staff and other agents; published figures are rare.
Recruiting players

Finding players

Your first clients will be players nobody else has noticed, or nobody else is serving well.

Where to look

  • Under-18, under-21 and reserve leagues. Go in person; few people do.
  • Lower divisions and semi-professional football, for late developers.
  • Players released by academies each spring. Clubs publish retained lists.
  • Players entering the last year, then the last six months, of a contract.
  • University and college football where it exists.
  • Players with dual nationality who could play for a second national team or avoid a foreign-player quota.
  • Players with an agent who has gone quiet. Respect the rules on exclusive agreements.

Tools

  • Transfermarkt for contract expiry dates, agents, career history. Treat values as rough.
  • FBref, Sofascore, FotMob for free match and season data.
  • Wyscout, Hudl, SciSports, TransferRoom for video and club-side platforms, when you can afford them.
  • The FIFA agent directory at agents.fifa.com to check who is licensed.

Judge the player and the person

  • Watch at least three full matches, one of them away or against a stronger side.
  • Name the level the player can reach in two years, and the clubs that play in a way that suits them.
  • Find out about character from coaches and teammates. Clubs will ask.
  • Ask who else is around the player: family, existing advisers, boot deals.

Minors: no approach before the six months leading up to the first professional contract age, and only with the guardian's written consent. No gifts or payments to players or families to sign with you. Check your national rules as well.

Recruiting players

Winning and signing a client

A player chooses an agent the way anyone chooses an adviser: someone who understands their situation and has a plan.

The first conversation

  • Open with what you saw in their game, in detail. It proves you watched.
  • Ask about their goals before talking about yourself.
  • Be honest about your experience. Sell attention and work rate, which large agencies cannot give a fringe player.
  • Include the family for young players. They often decide.

Bring a plan, in writing

  • Where the player is now, with evidence.
  • A realistic target for the next window and the next two years.
  • The clubs you would approach and why each fits.
  • What you will do each month: video, data profile, contact with clubs, check-ins.
  • Your fee and exactly when it becomes payable.

The representation agreement

Have a sports lawyer draft your template once. It is cheaper than one dispute.

Rules of approach

  • Ask first whether they have an agent. If the agreement is exclusive, the FFAR as written only lets you approach in its last two months. Leave your details and wait.
  • Minors. No contact with the player or the family until six months before the age for a first professional contract, with the guardian's written consent, after your minors CPD course.
  • Club and academy rules. Many academies and national associations restrict agent access to youth players and premises. Learn the local rules before you stand on a touchline.
  • No inducements. No cash, boots, phones or jobs for relatives in exchange for a signature.
  • Only you. Unlicensed scouts or friends may not make the approach for you.

What puts players off

  • Promising a specific club or a trial you cannot deliver.
  • Criticising their current agent.
  • Pushing for a signature at the first meeting.
  • Generic messages sent to the whole squad. Players compare screenshots.
Recruiting players

Building relationships that last

Signing a player takes months. Keeping him when a bigger agency calls takes years of doing what you said you would.

A realistic timeline

  1. Weeks 1–4: be useful. Watch matches. Send one honest observation after a game. Share a clip with a note on what a higher-level scout would look for.
  2. Months 2–3: meet properly. Coffee with the player, then with the family. Listen for their goals, worries and who else advises them.
  3. Month 3–4: present the plan. A written career plan with target level, clubs that fit and what you will do monthly.
  4. Then: the agreement. Give them the draft to take away, advise independent legal advice in writing, and let them come back to you.

What trust is made of

  • Honesty about level. Tell a League Two player he is a League Two player with a route to the Championship, and show the evidence. Flattery wins meetings and loses clients.
  • Small promises kept. If you say you will send clips on Monday, send them on Monday.
  • Availability. Answer the phone after a bad game, an injury, a row with the manager.
  • Transparency on money. Show the player every offer and exactly what you earn from each.
  • Respect for the family. Keep parents informed without talking over the player.

Working with families

  • Work out who really decides. It may be a mother, an older brother, a former coach.
  • Expect questions about school, housing and what happens if it does not work out. Have answers.
  • A relative who wants to act as the agent needs a licence like anyone else. Offer to work alongside a licensed family member rather than against them.
  • Respect culture and language. For players moving countries, a contact who speaks the family's language is worth more than any brochure.

Keeping clients when bigger agencies call

  • It will happen as soon as your player does well. Assume it and talk about it early.
  • Your defence is service, the family relationship and results, not the contract.
  • Renew conversations well before the two-year term ends. Review the plan together every six months.
  • If a player needs reach you do not have, propose a written co-operation with a larger agency for that deal. Sharing a fee beats losing the client.
  • If a client leaves, part well. Football is small and players come back, or send others.

Your recruitment pipeline

Keep a simple tracker and review it weekly: name, club, contract end, agent status, last contact, next step. Aim for 30 players watched, 10 in conversation, 3 close to signing. Most new agents fail by chasing one player at a time.

Dealing with clubs

Approaching clubs

Clubs get hundreds of player offers. The ones that get read solve a problem the club already has.

Find the right person

  • Sporting director or head of recruitment decides at most professional clubs.
  • Chief scout and scouts filter. A scout who rates your player is your best advocate inside.
  • Head coach matters most at smaller clubs.
  • Academy manager for young players.

Find names through club websites, LinkedIn, press conferences and people you meet at matches. A warm introduction beats any cold email.

Do the club's homework first

  • Squad list by position, age and contract end. Where is the gap next summer?
  • How the team plays, and the profile the coach uses in that position.
  • Who they signed in the last three windows, from which leagues, at what age and price.
  • Their constraints: budget level, foreign-player slots, work permits.

Working for clubs

Clubs also hire agents to find a buyer for a player or to source a profile. Get the mandate in writing: which player, which territories, exclusive or not, how long, and the fee. Without it you have no claim.

Dealing with clubs

How a deal runs

The steps are the same from a third-division free transfer to a record signing. Only the numbers change.

  1. Interest. A club asks about availability and price. Confirm the player wants to hear it.
  2. Club to club. If the player is under contract, the buying club must approach the current club in writing first. Fee, instalments, add-ons and sell-on are agreed between clubs.
  3. Personal terms. You negotiate the player's contract.
  4. Checks. Medical, work permit, registration slot. These come before signature, because a contract cannot be conditional on them.
  5. Paperwork. Transfer agreement, employment contract, your representation agreement and fee terms. You are named in the contract.
  6. Registration. The club enters the deal in TMS, the ITC is requested, the player is registered inside the window.
  7. Afterwards. Training rewards are calculated. Your fee is paid on the agreed schedule. You help the player settle.

Personal terms checklist

  • Length, and any option to extend. Who holds the option?
  • Basic salary, gross or net, currency, payment dates.
  • Signing-on fee and when it is paid.
  • Bonuses: appearances, goals, clean sheets, promotion, survival, European qualification.
  • Changes on promotion or relegation.
  • Release or buy-out clause, and sell-on share for the player where local rules allow.
  • Image rights.
  • Housing, car, flights, schooling, language lessons.
  • Injury and insurance terms.

Negotiating

  • Know the player's alternative. Two interested clubs is leverage; one is hope.
  • Know the club's pay structure. An offer above their ceiling ends the talk.
  • Trade across items. A club fixed on salary may move on length, bonuses or a release clause.
  • Agree your own fee early and in writing, so it never becomes a bargaining chip against your client.
  • The player decides. Present every offer, with your advice.

Dealing with clubs

Getting in the door at top clubs

A new agent will not get a sporting director of a Champions League club on the phone. There are routes that do work.

Five routes in

  • The scout who covers your league. Every big club has a scout or regional head for your market. They attend the same matches as you and they want reliable local information. This is the most realistic first contact.
  • The loans or emerging-talent department. More approachable than the first-team operation, and they deal with young players' agents all day.
  • An established agent. Agents with a relationship at the club can present your player under a written co-operation agreement. You share the fee and gain the introduction.
  • A club mandate. A selling club gives you written authority to find a buyer in a territory. Now you are calling on the club's behalf with something concrete.
  • Club marketplaces and events. TransferRoom lets clubs post the profiles they want and has an access programme for agents. Industry summits put decision-makers and agents in one room.

When the club calls you

  • Confirm you represent the player and can prove it. Send the agreement if asked.
  • Give straight answers on contract length, release clause, current salary band and the player's interest.
  • Do not invent rival offers. Recruitment staff talk to each other.
  • Tell the player the same day.
  • Ask about the process and the timeline, and who else is involved on their side.

Negotiating with a bigger club

  • They have done this hundreds of times. Take a sports lawyer with you for your first major contract.
  • Top clubs have wage structures by squad role. Ask where your player sits in it rather than naming a number first.
  • Push for what matters to a young player: a pathway to minutes, loan terms, pay rises tied to appearances, a sensible release or review clause.
  • Get your own fee agreed in writing early and keep it visible to your client.
  • Never leak to the press to force a deal. It closes that door for years.

Being an agent clubs want to deal with

  • Reply within the hour during a window.
  • Only offer players who fit what the club actually buys.
  • Say no when your player is not interested, quickly and politely.
  • Keep your word after the deal: no reopening terms, no surprise demands at signing.
Dealing with clubs

Case studies

Five well-known careers and what an agent can take from each. Fees are as widely reported in the press and are approximate.

Moisés Caicedo: an undervalued market and a stepping stone

Brighton signed Caicedo from Independiente del Valle in Ecuador in early 2021 for a few million pounds, loaned him to Beerschot in Belgium, then brought him into the Premier League. In 2023 Chelsea paid a British record fee reported above £100m.

  • Data-led clubs buy early from leagues others ignore. Know which clubs shop in your market.
  • A loan in a stepping-stone league is often part of the plan. Prepare your player for it.

Kaoru Mitoma: the work permit decides the route

Brighton bought Mitoma from Kawasaki Frontale in 2021. He did not yet qualify for a UK work permit, so he spent a season at Union Saint-Gilloise in Belgium, a club with the same owner, before joining Brighton's squad.

  • Eligibility rules shape deals as much as talent. Learn the work-permit system of every market you sell into.
  • Multi-club groups can solve problems a single club cannot. Map who owns what.

Erling Haaland: a ladder, with release clauses

Molde, then Red Bull Salzburg in 2019, Borussia Dortmund in January 2020, Manchester City in 2022. Advised by his father and the agent Mino Raiola, he chose clubs where he would start, and the Dortmund and City moves were both reported to have gone through release clauses.

  • Pick the club where the player plays, at each step. Minutes build value.
  • A release clause agreed on the way in is leverage on the way out.

Jude Bellingham: a family-led decision

In 2020, aged 17, Bellingham left Birmingham City for Borussia Dortmund, with bigger English clubs reported to be interested. His parents managed the process and the choice was built around first-team football and development.

  • For young players the family is the client relationship. An agent who cannot work with parents will not be in the room.
  • A clear development argument can beat a bigger name and a bigger wage.

Kim Min-jae: stepping through leagues

Jeonbuk in Korea, Beijing Guoan, Fenerbahçe in 2021, Napoli in 2022 where he won Serie A, then Bayern Munich in 2023 through a reported release clause of around €50m.

  • Each league proved him to the next buyer. Turkey was the bridge from Asia to a top-five league.
  • Short, performance-led steps can move faster than waiting for one dream transfer.

Jorge Mendes and Wolves: the relationship is with the club

After Fosun bought Wolverhampton Wanderers in 2016, a run of Mendes clients arrived, including the coach Nuno Espírito Santo and players such as Rúben Neves and Diogo Jota, and the club was promoted and reached Europe. Fosun held a stake in Mendes's agency, and the English Football League looked at the relationship and took no action.

  • The strongest agents are trusted advisers to owners and sporting directors, not sellers of one player.
  • Close ties attract scrutiny. Conflict-of-interest rules exist for this reason, so document everything.

Going global

How the global market works

Players move along predictable routes. An agent who understands the routes knows where a player can realistically go next.

The size of it

FIFA's International Transfer Snapshot for 1 June to 2 September 2026 reported a record of about USD 9.89bn in fees across more than 12,500 international transfers in men's football. English clubs spent the most, about USD 3.02bn, then Italy at about USD 1.1bn. French clubs received the most, about USD 1.7bn.

Most of those 12,500 moves involved no fee at all. The bulk of the market is free agents and loans at modest salaries, and that is where new agents work.

The chain

  1. Source markets. Countries that produce more players than their leagues can pay: Brazil, Argentina, France, West Africa, the Balkans, increasingly Japan, Ecuador and Colombia.
  2. Stepping-stone leagues. Belgium, the Netherlands, Portugal, Austria, Denmark, Switzerland, Turkey. They buy young, give minutes and European exposure, and sell on.
  3. Destination leagues. England, Spain, Germany, Italy, France, and for wages Saudi Arabia.

Your player's next move is usually one link along this chain, not a jump to the end of it.

What blocks a move

  • Work permits. England uses a points system (GBE) based on international caps, the quality of the league the player comes from, minutes and continental football. Clubs get a few exception slots. Other countries have salary thresholds or simpler rules.
  • Foreign-player quotas. Spain limits non-EU players per squad, Italy limits non-EU signings from abroad, Turkey, Saudi Arabia and most Asian leagues cap foreigners. Numbers change most seasons.
  • Passports. A second nationality can remove a quota problem. Check ancestry early.
  • Registration windows. They differ by country. A closed window in the buying country ends the deal, while an open one elsewhere creates late options.
  • Minors. Under-18s cannot move internationally outside the RSTP exceptions.
  • Calendars. Leagues on a calendar-year season are mid-season in the European summer, which changes price and availability.
  • National service and local law. Military service in Korea is the best-known example.

Costs that sit on top of the fee

  • Training compensation and the 5% solidarity contribution.
  • Sell-on clauses owed to former clubs.
  • Tax: net salary differs sharply between countries. Clubs think in gross, players in net.
  • Agent fees, and national rules on who may pay them.
Check before you promise

Before you tell a player a move is possible, confirm the work permit, the quota slot and the window with the buying club's football operations staff.

How to learn a foreign market in a month

Going global

Where the market is moving

A reading of the market in late 2026. This is the part of the course that will date fastest, so check it against the latest window.

Japan

One count put 114 Japanese players at European clubs at the start of 2025/26, and their share of foreign players in the English Championship has risen sharply. England moved the J1 League up a band in its work-permit points table for 2026/27, and the J.League has shifted to an autumn-to-spring season, which lines its windows up with Europe.

For an agent: demand is ahead of supply of people who understand both sides. Language and a Japanese partner are the barrier to entry.

Ecuador, Colombia and the smaller South American leagues

European clubs, Belgian ones in particular, now buy directly from these leagues at prices well below Brazil and Argentina. Independiente del Valle's academy is the model.

For an agent: an early relationship with a well-run academy is worth more than chasing individual players.

Belgium as a hub

Belgian clubs sign young players from South America, Japan, Scandinavia and Africa and sell them to the top five leagues. Many are owned by foreign groups.

For an agent: the most useful single league to understand if you move players into Europe.

France as a seller

French clubs received more transfer income than any other country in summer 2026. Broadcast income has been under pressure, so clubs sell young players earlier.

For an agent: a huge pool of talent and motivated sellers, behind a national licensing system you need a French partner to work in.

The English Football League

Championship and League One clubs recruit abroad far more than five years ago, helped by work-permit exception slots. For 2026/27 those slots are limited to players born in 2003 or later.

For an agent: a realistic buyer for good young players from second-tier European leagues and Asia.

North America after the World Cup

The 2026 World Cup put the market in view, and 267 players who appeared at it changed clubs that summer for a combined USD 3.3bn. MLS has decided to move to a summer-to-spring season, bringing its calendar closer to Europe's.

For an agent: a buyer for players aged 18 to 22 and a growing seller, with roster rules that reward specialists.

Saudi Arabia

Still a wage-led buyer, with more interest in players in their early and mid twenties than in 2023.

For an agent: strong salaries for clients; rely on established intermediaries and a lawyer.

Women's football

FIFA reported a record USD 28.6m in international fees in the mid-2026 window, more than double the previous one, across 1,406 transfers. English clubs spent the most and Swedish clubs received the most.

For an agent: the fastest-growing segment with the fewest specialist agents. Fees are small today and relationships formed now will compound.

Structural shifts to watch

  • Multi-club ownership. Groups move players between their clubs. Know the groups in your market.
  • Data-led recruitment. Clubs find players without agents' help. Your value shifts to information data cannot give: character, intentions, availability.
  • The 2027 RSTP. New rules on breach and compensation may make exits more negotiable, and lower-paid players gain a share of fixed transfer fees.
  • Agent regulation. The fee cap and other FFAR rules are still unsettled after the EU court rulings of July 2026.
Keep this current

Twice a year, read FIFA's transfer snapshot after each window and the CIES Football Observatory reports. Note which countries your target league bought from. Update your own list of three markets to work.

Going global

Finding partners abroad

Nobody knows every market. International agents work through a network of local partners, and a good one is the difference between a deal and a wasted flight.

What a local partner gives you

  • Access to club decision-makers who do not answer strangers.
  • Knowledge of salaries, quotas, tax and which clubs pay on time.
  • Language, and presence at matches you cannot attend.
  • Compliance with local agent rules, where a domestic licence or registration is needed.

Who they are

  • A licensed agent in that country. The standard partner. Only licensed agents may be paid for agent services.
  • A sports lawyer. For contracts, tax and disputes. Paid a fee, not a share.
  • Scouts and former players. For information and introductions. They cannot approach players or negotiate for you unless licensed.

How to find them

  • FIFA agent directory, filtered by country.
  • Transfermarkt: look up who represents players at the clubs you are targeting. Those agents have the relationships.
  • Agents who have moved players along your corridor before. Check the last three seasons of transfers.
  • Events and marketplaces where agents from many countries meet.
  • Ask club staff which local agents they trust.

Check them before you share anything

Put the co-operation in writing

Co-operation agreement: points to cover
PARTIES         Both agents, with FIFA licence numbers
SCOPE           Named player(s) or named club mandate
TERRITORY       Country or clubs the partner may approach
EXCLUSIVITY     Exclusive or not, within that territory
TERM            Usually one window or until a set date
ROLES           Who speaks to the player, who speaks to clubs
CLIENT          Who holds the representation agreement / mandate
FEE SPLIT       Percentage each, on fees actually received
INVOICING       Who invoices the club or player, when the other is paid
COSTS           Each bears own costs unless agreed in writing
CLUB LIST       Clubs each side has already contacted (dated)
NON-CIRCUMVENT  No direct dealing with the other's client or contact
CONFIDENTIALITY Player data, salary information, club terms
COMPLIANCE      Both remain licensed; FFAR and national rules apply
LAW / DISPUTES  Governing law and forum
Have a sports lawyer turn this into a contract before you use it.

Fee splits are negotiated deal by deal. An even split is a common starting point when one side brings the player and the other brings the club.

First message to a potential partner
Dear [Name],

I am a FIFA licensed agent based in [country], working mainly in
[league/level]. I saw that you represent [player] at [club] and have
placed players with [clubs] in [country].

Would you be open to a call about co-operating on this player in

Kind regards,
[Name] · licence no. [ ] · [phone]

Making partnerships work

  • Start with one player and one window. Expand after a deal is done and paid.
  • Give as well as take. Bring your partner opportunities in your market.
  • Keep a dated record of which clubs each of you contacted. Most disputes are about who introduced whom.
  • Keep the player informed about who is working for him and why.
  • Never let a partner sign your client to a new agreement. The client relationship stays with you.
Business

Looking after clients

Players leave agents who appear only at contract time. Service between deals is what keeps clients and wins referrals.

  • Regular contact. A weekly message, a monthly call, a visit each season. Watch their matches.
  • Career planning. Review minutes, role and progress every few months against the plan you wrote.
  • Bad times. Injury, loss of form, being dropped. This is when an agent earns loyalty.
  • Moving. Housing, banking, schools, language, family settling in.
  • Specialists. Refer to qualified lawyers, accountants and financial advisers. Do not give tax or investment advice yourself, and take no kickbacks.
  • Profile. Sensible social media, boot and commercial deals where they exist.
  • After football. Coaching badges, education, savings. Start the conversation early.

Conflicts of interest

  • Two clients competing for one position at one club: tell both.
  • A club offering to pay you more if the player takes less: refuse, and tell the player.
  • Acting for the club and the player in one deal needs written consent from both under the FFAR as written.
Business

Setting up and staying compliant

Small administrative failures suspend licences. Set the routine up once.

Set-up

  • Register with your national association if it requires it, in addition to the FIFA licence.
  • Decide between sole trader and company with an accountant. The licence stays personal either way.
  • A separate business bank account and proper invoices.
  • Professional indemnity insurance where available.
  • Template documents drafted by a lawyer: representation agreement, club mandate, co-operation agreement.

Each year

  • Pay the licence fee before the deadline.
  • Complete the CPD credits on the FIFA Agent Platform.
  • Keep the platform up to date: agreements, agency details, changes in your circumstances.
  • Re-check eligibility before taking any role or shareholding in football.

Scams to recognise

  • "Agents" who charge players for trials. Legitimate clubs do not sell trials.
  • Invitation letters from clubs sent from free email accounts.
  • Intermediaries with "a mandate from a big club" and nothing in writing.
  • Requests to pay a registration or visa fee to release a contract.

If you see it, protect your player and say so. Your reputation is the business.

Business

Network and first 90 days

You can start all of this before you pass. None of it needs a licence except approaching players and offering them to clubs.

Building a network

  • Be at matches. Scouts, coaches and other agents stand in the same places every week.
  • Help people before you need them: a tip on a player, an introduction.
  • Find a mentor. An experienced agent who lets you watch one deal teaches more than a course.
  • Go to industry events and courses for the people as much as the content.
  • Write about your niche. A weekly post on your league makes club staff recognise your name.
